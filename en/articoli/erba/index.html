The Erba massacre

How the evidence converged in court and why competing accounts persist

Classification: Real case / Documentary analysis

Case status: Court proceedings concluded

Level of certainty: High in the court proceedings, disputed in public accounts

Method applied: Bottino System (MRN)

Tags: Erba, Olindo Romano, Rosa Bazzi, Mario Frigerio, Erba massacre, court reporting, documentary analysis

Update: The legal position remains unchanged. In March 2025, Italy’s Court of Cassation dismissed an appeal against the refusal to reopen the case. The life sentences imposed on Olindo Romano and Rosa Bazzi remain in force. In its October 2024 reasons for refusing a review, the Brescia Court of Appeal had already found that the evidence cited by the defence was neither new nor capable of overturning the finding of guilt. Subsequent developments have not reopened the proceedings.

The final convictions and alternative accounts of the killings must be considered separately. Public debate may continue, but it cannot in itself alter the courts’ decisions.

The Erba massacre ended in final convictions, yet it remains the subject of public debate. On 11 December 2006, four people were killed in an apartment building where relations between neighbours had long been strained. The victims were Raffaella Castagna, young Youssef Marzouk, Valeria Cherubini and Paola Galli. The scene bore the marks of extreme violence and of a fire set after the attack. From the outset, investigators faced both a multiple killing and an altered crime scene.

The proceedings established several facts. The victims were attacked inside the flat. The fire came afterwards, in circumstances consistent with the destruction or impairment of evidence. Mario Frigerio was the sole survivor, and his testimony would play a central role. This matters because the reconstruction rests on more than physical evidence. Witness statements, confessions and corroborating findings are closely intertwined.

The prosecution’s case soon centred on Olindo Romano and Rosa Bazzi. The trial brought together the early confessions and their subsequent retraction, relations between the neighbours, the survivor’s testimony, physical evidence and the reconstruction of the events as a whole. The judges assessed these elements together, without making the reconstruction depend on any single finding.

The confessions are among the most difficult pieces of evidence to assess. Two defendants describing the crime carried considerable weight. Their subsequent retractions immediately raised a problem that would persist for years. Were the confessions spontaneous or induced? Did they have independent evidential value, or could they be assessed only alongside other findings? Were they central to the proof of guilt, or did they call for particular caution? These questions fuelled much of the later dispute over how the case should be understood.

Alongside the confessions, Mario Frigerio’s testimony carried considerable weight. As the sole survivor, he was the only direct witness to the attack. His identification of the attackers became a cornerstone of the prosecution’s case. It also became a focus of dispute. Questions concerned his physical condition, his memory, the possibility of influence, the time that had elapsed and what he could perceive during an act of extreme violence. As in other high-profile cases, one of the strongest pieces of evidence became the point on which interpretations diverged.

The proceedings nevertheless reached a settled conclusion. The convictions became final, bringing the court case to a close. That finding cannot be set aside in any account of the case: the courts established the responsibility of Olindo Romano and Rosa Bazzi. Yet it was after the proceedings ended that the case took on an unusual second life. Applications for review, new expert opinions, fresh readings of the evidence and sustained media attention kept the story alive outside the courtroom.

The distinction between the final judgment and the debate that followed is essential. Several elements that the judges found consistent supported the same conclusion. Meanwhile, competing accounts persisted. They have not altered the judgment, but for some members of the public they continue to undermine the sense that the matter is settled. This shows how a formally closed case can remain the subject of a symbolic dispute.

In structure, the Erba case differs both from open cases in which the evidence does not converge and from those that depend entirely on one dominant piece of evidence. What matters here is how the elements fit together. Confessions, retractions, testimony, the sequence of events and the surrounding circumstances support one another in the court’s reconstruction. The alternative account challenges that relationship, calling into question the coherence of the whole rather than any one finding.

This is why the Erba massacre still holds such a prominent place in public memory. Beyond the violence of the killings, the case shows with particular clarity the distance between the stability of a final judgment and the instability of the story told about it. The judgment stands. It is final and has not been overturned. Yet accounts of the case continue to provoke disagreement and symbolically reopen what the courts have closed. That divide, even more than the account of the massacre itself, explains why the Erba case endures.
