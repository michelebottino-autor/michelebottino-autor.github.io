Jack the Ripper

The first modern case of an unsolved series of murders

Classification: Historical case / Documentary analysis

Case status: Historically unresolved, with no conclusive judicial finding

Level of certainty: High that the murders took place, low as to the identity of the killer

Method applied: Bottino System (MRN)

Tags: Jack the Ripper, Whitechapel, serial killer, London 1888, canonical victims, historical case, documentary analysis

Update: The case has never been conclusively resolved. Theories, attributions and calls for a fresh historical assessment continue to emerge. These include renewed interest in Aaron Kosminski and a request in 2025 by a descendant of one victim for a new inquest. None of these initiatives has produced the evidence or official finding needed for a definitive solution or a judicial determination.

The Whitechapel murders are documented. None of the identifications proposed over the years has established the killer’s identity with certainty. This article distinguishes witness accounts from later reconstructions.

In 1888 a series of murders in Whitechapel, in London’s East End, drew the attention of the police and the press. More than a century later, the identity of the person responsible for the killings attributed to Jack the Ripper remains unproven.

Between August and November that year, five women were killed who are traditionally known as the canonical victims: Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes and Mary Jane Kelly. The killings share some features, but there are also differences that call for caution in attributing them all to one person.

Whitechapel was then marked by poverty and overcrowding. Social conditions, the investigative resources of the period and mounting press pressure complicated the collection and assessment of witness accounts. Both the Metropolitan Police and the City of London Police took part in the investigations.

Numerous suspects have been considered over the years, including Montague John Druitt, Aaron Kosminski and George Chapman. None of these proposed identifications has established who committed the murders.

Letters sent to newspapers and the police helped make the case famous. The best known, the “Dear Boss” letter, popularised the name Jack the Ripper. The authenticity of many of these communications is disputed. Without corroboration, they cannot be treated as the killer’s own words.

The murders are documented, but there was no conclusive judicial finding. Later reconstructions must therefore be distinguished from information gathered at the time. The case endures in popular culture partly because of the distance between what is known and what remains conjecture.
