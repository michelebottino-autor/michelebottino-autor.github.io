The Garlasco murder

The final judgment, the circumstantial evidence, the renewed investigation and recent theories about the killing

Classification: Real case / Documentary analysis

Case status: Final conviction, with a separate investigation continuing at the time described

Level of certainty: Mixed

Method applied: Bottino System (MRN)

Tags: Garlasco, Chiara Poggi, Alberto Stasi, Andrea Sempio, court reporting, documentary analysis, renewed investigation, house alarm, protected files

Documented developments and reported hypotheses, January–April 2026: According to court reports, on 29 January 2026 the preliminary investigations judge in Pavia rejected a request by Andrea Sempio’s defence team for a pretrial evidentiary hearing concerning the computers of Alberto Stasi and Chiara Poggi. In March 2026, the press carried advance reports of a forensic medical assessment. In April, further reports discussed theories about how the attacker entered the house and about digital material. These were accounts published in the press. This article does not present them as findings of a court. Alberto Stasi’s final conviction is separate from both the subsequent investigation and the theories reported in the media. This summary covers reports from the period indicated and does not establish the present status of the investigation.

The final conviction must be considered separately from the subsequent investigation. A new inquiry does not in itself reopen a final judgment.

The Garlasco murder took a complex course through the courts. Successive courts assessed the same evidence differently, culminating in a final conviction based on circumstantial evidence. Chiara Poggi was killed on 13 August 2007 at her family home in Garlasco. Her boyfriend, Alberto Stasi, raised the alarm. Over the years the case went through acquittals, rulings being set aside, convictions and applications for review. More recently, renewed investigative work brought it back to public attention. Reading the case files and judgments requires a distinction between the courts’ findings and interpretations circulated in the media. Public debate has not always observed that distinction.

The investigation did not lead directly to a conviction. Alberto Stasi was acquitted at first instance in 2009, and the acquittal was upheld on appeal in 2011. In 2013, the Court of Cassation set that judgment aside and ordered a retrial. Stasi was convicted on retrial in 2014. The Court of Cassation upheld the conviction in 2015, making it final. Its written reasons were filed in 2016, setting out the basis for that conclusion. A comparison of the rulings shows how much turned on the assessment of the evidence. Many of the physical findings stayed the same. What changed was the weight each carried, its significance and its place in the court’s reasoning as a whole.

The final conviction did not rest on any single conclusive piece of evidence. No murder weapon was found. The motive was never fully explained. No single finding could dispel every doubt on its own. The conviction rested instead on a mosaic of circumstantial evidence, assessed as a whole at the retrial. One key issue was the timing of events on the morning of the killing, particularly the interval between the house alarm being switched off and activity on the computer. The retrial court regarded that interval as consistent with the time needed for the killing and as a difficulty for the alibi. Another issue was a black women’s bicycle seen near the house at a time consistent with the killing. Both the sighting and the way it was dealt with in the investigation and in statements mattered. Other elements included the victim’s DNA on the pedals of a bicycle used by Stasi, the bathroom soap dispenser, footwear marks, and inconsistencies in accounts of the call to the 118 emergency service and the discovery of the body. Individually these elements were insufficient. At the retrial the court found that, taken together, they supported a coherent account.

This is probably the most important aspect of the case, and the one least understood in public discussion. Many accounts suggest that entirely new and decisive evidence emerged over time. Often the physical evidence had been there all along. Between the acquittals and the conviction, what changed was its interpretation. The soap dispenser, for instance, figured in the case from the outset. The courts that acquitted Stasi found that the evidence from it could not be dated with useful precision. In 2014 the retrial court read it as part of a sequence consistent with someone washing after the killing. The same applies to the DNA on the bicycle pedals. Initially the emphasis was on the inability to date the trace. Later it carried different weight when considered alongside other findings. The turning point was how the evidence was assessed as a whole.

The Court of Cassation’s 2013 ruling marked the turning point. It identified no new evidence. It challenged the way the earlier courts had assessed the circumstantial evidence. Its point was that testing each item in isolation was insufficient. The court also had to ask whether several items, read together, pointed to the same conclusion. That change of approach paved the way for the conviction at the retrial. Findings previously considered insufficient became, when assessed together, the basis of a reconstruction the court found sound.

The Court of Cassation’s 2015 ruling made the 16-year sentence final. Its written reasons, filed in 2016, reinforced the conclusion reached at the retrial that guilt had been established beyond reasonable doubt. Later attempts to reopen the case by seeking a review did not overturn the judgment. That closed the first phase of the case in court. Questions about how the sentence was to be served, including work outside prison and partial release, came later. The conviction itself stood.

The case continued to develop. A new phase of investigation focusing on Andrea Sempio began in 2025. There is particular scope for confusion here. At the time described, two distinct matters were in play. The first was the final judicial finding concerning Alberto Stasi, reached between 2013 and 2015 and upheld by the Court of Cassation. The second was the renewed investigation concerning Sempio. It involved genetic analyses, the mark known as “print 33”, discussion of DNA profiles under the victim’s fingernails and questions about the unidentified profile known as “Ignoto 3”. More recently it also involved fresh interpretations of how the attacker entered the house and of digital material kept by Chiara Poggi. These matters have different evidential and legal standing.

On the material publicly available at the time, the renewed investigation had yielded no new finding by a court. Technical inquiries were still under way. The same qualification applies to theories reported more recently in the press: that Chiara might not have willingly opened the door to the killer, a fresh interpretation of the alarm being switched off, the idea that the attack had been prepared from outside, and questions about a protected folder reportedly transferred to a USB stick that later went missing. These matters could guide further checks. They did not carry the weight of a final judgment or amount to established conclusions.

Conflating the judgment on Stasi, the renewed investigation concerning Sempio and recent media theories would obscure the most important distinction in the case. A settled judicial finding would be mixed with matters still being examined, some known only through press reports. Here the limits of recent coverage have been especially clear. Incomplete data, probabilistic results, fragments of technical assessments and untested reconstructions have often been treated as proof or decisive breakthroughs. Print 33, DNA under the fingernails, possible contamination during the autopsy in relation to “Ignoto 3”, a new interpretation of the alarm, and reports of files missing from the victim’s computer have attracted considerable attention. The public record on each remains incomplete. Until the full technical reports and any use made of them in court are available, these questions must remain open.

Once years of speculation are set aside, six points remain. First, Chiara Poggi was killed in her home on 13 August 2007. Second, Alberto Stasi was acquitted twice and then convicted by a judgment that became final, after the Court of Cassation required a different approach to the circumstantial evidence. Third, the conviction rested on several findings taken together. Fourth, many much-debated items of evidence did not physically change over time. Their weight and significance varied with the way they were interpreted. Fifth, at the time described, the new investigation consisted of ongoing technical checks and hypotheses, without a new judicial determination. Sixth, the latest reports about the house alarm and the victim’s digital files, significant as they were to the investigation and its public retelling, did not amount to an established finding by a court.

The Garlasco murder continues to provoke debate because of its gravity and because it challenges a simple expectation: that justice should follow a straight path to a clear and intuitive truth. Here the proceedings showed how decisive the method of assessing circumstantial evidence can be. They also showed how public accounts can distort or simplify what the record shows, or anticipate conclusions it does not yet support. That is why Garlasco remains more than a news story. It tests the limits of the relationship between evidence, interpretation, judgment and narrative.
