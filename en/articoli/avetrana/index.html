The Avetrana murder

How the courts established the facts, piece by piece

Michele Bottino

Italian original

English translation of the Italian original. Time references and assessments reflect the source article, not a new verification of the proceedings.

Classification: Real case / Documentary analysis

Case status: Final judgment

Level of certainty: High for the judicial findings. The accounts of events vary in reliability.

Method applied: Bottino System (MRN)

Tags: Avetrana, Sarah Scazzi, Sabrina Misseri, Cosima Serrano, Michele Misseri, Court reporting, documentary analysis

The judgment stands. Recent developments concern the public and cultural reception of the case. In October 2024 the Taranto court provisionally suspended the series about the case over the use of “Avetrana” in its title. The original title was reinstated in March 2025. The fifteenth anniversary in August 2025 brought renewed public attention to the case. The final judgment stands.

This account distinguishes facts established in court from accounts circulated in public debate. Later hypotheses alone do not alter final judgments.

On 26 August 2010, in Avetrana, a fifteen-year-old girl leaves home to meet her cousin. She will never return.

Sarah Scazzi’s case drew intense public attention, partly because of the contradictory statements and the different weight given to the evidence gathered during the investigation.

The proceedings eventually reached a final conclusion, upheld by Italy’s Court of Cassation in 2017. The decision rested on an assessment of the statements alongside the other evidence.

What, then, did the courts settle? Sarah Scazzi died by strangulation. The forensic finding was mechanical asphyxia caused by constriction. The place was established too. The killing took place inside the Misseri home, not in the garage. The distinction matters because it separates the killing from what happened afterwards. The time of death was placed between 2.00 and 2.25 p.m. The entire reconstruction turns on those twenty-five minutes. The courts upheld the convictions of Sabrina Misseri and Cosima Serrano for the killing. Michele Misseri’s conviction for disposing of the body also stood. These are the firm points. Much else in the Avetrana case is harder to follow.

One feature that marked the case was the succession of statements by Michele Misseri. At first he said he had killed the girl in the garage. Then he named his daughter Sabrina as the person responsible. He changed the details again, shifting the roles of those involved, before once more claiming responsibility himself. These were more than adjustments. The accounts contradicted one another. They could neither all be true nor be reconciled into one coherent narrative. Here the case took an unusual turn. A confession, often central to a trial, lost its primacy. The courts examined it piece by piece and relied on it only where other evidence was consistent with it.

Another crucial question concerned Sarah’s phone. During a call at 2.42.48 p.m., it connected to a cell serving the area around the garage. For a long time this seemed potentially decisive. If the phone was in the garage, perhaps the killing had taken place there. Technical analysis, however, showed that a phone inside the house could connect to the same cell. The connection was established, but its apparent precision fell away. It could not place the handset in either location to the exclusion of the other. The courts took the finding into account and gave it correspondingly less weight.

The sequence of events on 26 August 2010 can be reconstructed only in part. There are fixed points. Death occurred between 2.00 and 2.25 p.m. There was the 2.42 call, and a call at 3.08 suggests that the body was being dealt with at a later stage. What happened between those points is less clear. The court could reach a conclusion without accounting for every minute. Its findings leave gaps in the sequence.

This is what makes Avetrana particularly interesting. No confession, single clue or isolated technical finding decided the case. The conclusion emerged through a process of selection. Statements were examined and set aside where they failed to stand up. Technical findings were checked and their significance reassessed. Only the essential points in the chronology could be fixed. Once the incompatible elements had been removed, a solid core remained.

The Avetrana murder illustrates with unusual clarity how seldom judicial findings take the form of a straight narrative. Pieces of evidence do not always fit together at first. Here the final judgment did not adopt any one account in its entirety. The courts assembled a reconstruction by deciding which elements to retain and which to discard. More than a case remembered for a confession or a decisive clue, Avetrana is a case about how facts are established.
