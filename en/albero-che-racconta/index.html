The family tree that tells a story

Six units and eighteen lessons on writing a clear family history whose sources readers can trace and whose findings you can update, without merging namesakes or inventing family ties.

Teaching preview under review. Case G-01, people P01–P04 and sources D1–D3 are entirely fictional. No personal genealogical data are collected. There is no enrolment, payment, human assessment or certificate.

Worked case: two people with the same name

In simulated file G-01, fictional register D1 names “Andrea Serra”. Fictional letter D2 mentions an Andrea Serra from a different place. Nothing establishes that they are the same person. The reconstruction keeps two separate entries.

Rule: a name in a source does not by itself prove a person’s identity or family relationship.

Unit 1 of 6 · preview under review

The reader and the promise of the story

Objective: Define the audience, the question and the limits of a genealogical reconstruction.

1.1 A promise the records can support

A family history need not promise to reconstruct every life or relationship. Decide whom you are writing for and which question you can address using registers, letters and recollections attributed to named sources. In case G-01 the family and its records are entirely fictional.

1.2 Resemblance does not establish identity

A name occurring in two documents does not prove that both concern the same person. Record the place, date, role and any additional identifiers. Where these are missing, record two candidates rather than a single ancestor.

1.3 Set the boundaries of the narrative

Choose a starting generation, a span of years and a clearly stated geographical area. Leave room to revise the chronology when a new source emerges. Never fill gaps with invented dialogue presented as testimony.

Practical exercise

In fictional file G-01, two people named Andrea Serra appear in entries from different years. Frame a question that does not presuppose they are the same person. Create a record of the information still missing.

Unit 2 of 6 · preview under review

The thread between the branches

Objective: Map family relationships that the evidence supports, allows as possibilities or contradicts.

2.1 People and connections in the tree

Each person is a node with a unique identifier. Each family relationship is a link supported by one or more records or accounts. Do not enter a relationship as certain when its only basis is unverified oral tradition.

2.2 Two people named Andrea

In fictional case G-01, register D1 mentions Andrea Serra at the workshop. Letter D2 mentions an Andrea Serra in a different place, with no identifying details in common. Keep the two nodes separate until independent evidence connects them.

2.3 A tree that can change

Label each link as documented in a source, reported by a witness or inferred. Retain alternatives and the reasons for corrections. Even a simple diagram should show uncertainty without relying on colour.

Practical exercise

Draw up a text outline of four fictional people, coded P01–P04. Include one link explicitly attributed to a recollection, one documented in the case and one unverified relationship. Explain how the three differ.

Unit 3 of 6 · preview under review

Social setting and people

Objective: Describe the social context without assigning unproven roles or motives.

3.1 Dates in a family history

Distinguish the date a record was registered from the birth, marriage, journey or other event it describes. For a later document, state when it was drawn up and what earlier event it refers to.

3.2 Occupations and places

A business recorded at an address does not establish that every namesake worked there. Use maps and directories to provide context, making clear that context alone does not identify the person.

3.3 Giving people a voice without inventing one

Build the account from verifiable details: a document was drawn up, a person was named, a move was reported. Do not supply feelings, dialogue or intentions where the source is silent.

Practical exercise

Write 160–220 words for a chapter on fictional case G-01. Cite D1 and D2 without assuming that the two Andreas are one person. Add a short note on the limits of the reconstruction.

Unit 4 of 6 · preview under review

Sources and scholarly notes

Objective: Build a system of sources and citations that another reader can check.

4.1 Catalogue a source

For each item in your inventory, record its identifier, institution or custodian, shelfmark or location, page where applicable, date, kind of copy and description. A general reference to “family papers” cannot be checked.

4.2 A claims table

For each sentence in the chapter, record the source code, relevant extract and degree of certainty. A source attests that a name occurs in it. Identifying several occurrences as the same individual is a further conclusion.

4.3 Write clear critical notes

Flag contradictions, manuscripts not consulted, unchecked OCR transcriptions and second-hand testimony. With real sources, consider rights, privacy and the need to avoid publishing data about living people.

Practical exercise

Make a table of five claims about case G-01. For each, add a D1 or D2 reference, a degree of certainty and the check still required. Mark at least one statement as unsuitable for publication as fact.

Unit 5 of 6 · preview under review

Versions and corrections

Objective: Manage versions, corrections, publication and privacy.

5.1 Record each correction

Give every change to a family relationship a date, a reason and a documentary reference. Keep earlier versions so that you can explain to readers why the reconstruction changed.

5.2 Distinguish conflicting accounts

When two entries disagree, report both before choosing between them. Base your preference on evidence and explicit criteria, even if another solution would make a simpler diagram.

5.3 Protect living people

Do not publish exact dates, addresses or sensitive family ties concerning living people without an adequate basis and a privacy assessment. This exercise uses only fictional identities and asks for no real personal data.

Practical exercise

Simulate an update: a new note, D3, calls the P01–P03 relationship into question. Make a revision entry giving the previous version, new interpretation, reason, available sources and a note for the reader.

Unit 6 of 6 · preview under review

A pilot chapter

Objective: Plan the contents, first chapter and critical notes of a family history.

6.1 From the question to the evidence

Open the book with its aim, scope, a guide to the sources and your criteria for identifying people. In the chapters distinguish confirmed identities from proposed ones, and leave space for unresolved relationships.

6.2 A pilot chapter with notes

Write 350–500 words on G-01. Include a chronology of the accounts, a brief outline of the nodes, references to D1–D3 and a paragraph on what cannot yet be established.

6.3 Revision and reader feedback

Check names, codes, dates, notes and diagrams for consistency. Prepare three questions for a future comprehension test. Only report reader testing or independent reviews if they have actually taken place.

Practical exercise

Submit a six-section outline and the G-01 pilot chapter, with citations to the three fictional documents, a claims table and a record of unresolved questions. Do not publish data about real people.
