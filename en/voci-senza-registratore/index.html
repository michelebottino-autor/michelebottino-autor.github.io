Voices without a recorder

Six units and eighteen lessons on reconstructing second-hand memories from notes, letters and reported testimony without inventing words or claiming certainty.

Teaching preview under review. Case V-01 and its three sources N1, R1 and E1 are entirely fictional. No recordings, authentic testimony or private interviews are used. There is no enrolment, payment, independent review or certificate.

Case V-01: three sources and no recording

N1: a fictional note records a morning departure. R1: a fictional second-hand recollection places it in the evening. E1: an imaginary letter mentions a departure without giving a time. These three sources establish neither a definite chronology nor the exact words of the person in question.

Every detail was created for this lesson and refers to no real event or person.

Unit 1 of 6 · preview under review

First-hand and second-hand memories

Objective: Distinguish first-hand testimony, memories passed on by others and notes with no accompanying audio.

1.1 From speech to a written trace

A sentence in a notebook is not a recording. Record who heard it, when they wrote it down, and whether the wording was reconstructed or quoted from a verifiable text.

1.2 A memory at second hand

In fictional case V-01, someone reports what another person allegedly said years earlier. Do not present the reported words as first-hand testimony from the supposed original speaker.

1.3 Describing what the source leaves unsaid

If there is no audio, signed transcript or original recording, state that only a note survives. Quotation marks would misleadingly suggest that you could reproduce the spoken words.

Practical exercise

Make a record for the fictional case V-01 covering note N1, recollection R1 and letter E1. Distinguish the physical form of each source from its provenance and identify what cannot be verified.

Unit 2 of 6 · preview under review

Attribution and chains of testimony

Objective: Map how the account was transmitted and attribute each statement to its source.

2.1 Who said what

Trace the path from a hypothetical speaker to a later witness and then to the researcher’s note. At each step, details may be selected or misunderstood.

2.2 The source behind the source

A letter recounting a conversation establishes what the letter says. Whether the writer remembered the words accurately remains open. Give the identifier E1 and its fictional date.

2.3 When to use quotation marks

Use quotation marks only for words reproduced faithfully from a document. Introduce a paraphrased memory with wording such as “according to the account as reported”.

Practical exercise

Draw the hypothetical three-stage chain for V-01. Write one correctly attributed sentence and one you would reject because it wrongly presents words as direct speech.

Unit 3 of 6 · preview under review

Comparing versions

Objective: Compare conflicting accounts and identify where one may have influenced another.

3.1 A table of versions

Give each source a row. Record when it was created, who speaks, who writes it down, the event mentioned, proximity to that event and limitations. Keep incompatible versions separate.

3.2 Keeping contradictions visible

If N1 says the departure was in the morning and R1 places it in the evening, include both versions in V-01. Leave the exact time open.

3.3 Independent recollections

Two accounts are not independent if the second writer heard the first. Distinguish the number of documents from the number of independent chains of testimony.

Practical exercise

Make a table of the three fictional V-01 sources. Include at least one contradiction and one dependency between sources, and mark the details that remain unresolved.

Unit 4 of 6 · preview under review

Ethical responsibilities towards living people

Objective: Protect living people and their privacy, and check the terms of consent.

4.1 Interviews and permissions

For real material, check the purpose of collection, applicable consent, online dissemination and handling of personal information. A willingness to talk does not automatically include permission to publish.

4.2 Details that could identify someone

Omit unnecessary details that could identify living third parties. Do not attribute invented memories or unsubstantiated stories to real people.

4.3 Making corrections visible

If a witness corrects a note, retain a record of the previous version in a protected archive. Update the public text with an explanation proportionate to the correction.

Practical exercise

Prepare a checklist for using a real note. Cover consent, third-party data, storage, limits on reproduction and a way to submit corrections. Collect no real data in this exercise.

Unit 5 of 6 · preview under review

Making editorial choices transparent

Objective: Write a readable account without falsifying or dramatising the memory.

5.1 Opening with an attribution

Start with the document: “A fictional note records a departure.” Avoid “they remembered perfectly” if you cannot establish where the recollection came from or how accurate it was.

5.2 Transitions and sequence

Arrange the sources in documentary order. Avoid inventing an emotional progression. Make editorial transitions recognisable and keep them out of unrecorded dialogue.

5.3 Uncertainty as information

Explain uncertainties about times, names and origins in plain language. Use notes to keep claims traceable and the main text readable.

Practical exercise

Write 180–250 words on fictional case V-01. Include three references, to N1, R1 and E1, and a note on uncertainty. Invent no direct speech.

Unit 6 of 6 · preview under review

A narrative with notes

Objective: Submit a documentary account with a map of the testimony and explanatory notes.

6.1 Inventory and provenance

Include entries for N1, R1 and E1 with their fictional origin, date of writing and limitations. State that none is an authentic recording.

6.2 Documenting each claim

Link each event you describe to a source and attribute it correctly. For hypotheses, retain alternative explanations and note the checks still needed.

6.3 Review before publication

Check quotations, privacy, rights and clarity of attribution. Prepare questions for reader testing and accurately state which tests are still pending.

Practical exercise

Write a 350–450-word account and map the three sources in case V-01. Attach your register of claims and list the checks still outstanding.
